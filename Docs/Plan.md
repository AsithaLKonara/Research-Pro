## 3. What separates the winners from the rest?

The useful finding is not that Apple sells phones. It is that the top ten products serve several distinct demand patterns.

### Five patterns worth testing

1\. Multiple price points

Both premium and affordable models appear in the rankings. This suggests that a strong product portfolio can serve multiple customer segments rather than depending on one expensive flagship.

2\. Brand and ecosystem

Customers are not choosing specifications alone. Familiarity, compatibility, support, resale value and switching costs can influence their choices.

3\. Perceived value

Products do not have to be the cheapest. They need to make their price feel justified relative to the alternatives.

4\. Product lifecycle

New products can create launch demand, while older models remain competitive through price reductions and continued availability.

5\. Distribution and availability

Even a good product can underperform if customers cannot find it, afford it or obtain service for it. Wider availability can be as important as product features.

## 5. How we turn this into a research model for our own products

For future categories, we should maintain a consistent product scorecard. It allows us to compare products within the same category and later compare opportunities across categories.

## Product opportunity scorecard

Illustrative scoring framework. Scores must be calculated from collected evidence; no scores have been assigned to these phones without a complete dataset.

Demand

25% weight

Sales volume, search demand and category size

Growth momentum

20% weight

Year-over-year and quarterly changes

Customer value

15% weight

Reviews, satisfaction and recurring needs

Competition

15% weight

Number and strength of competing products

Profitability

15% weight

Costs, selling price and contribution margin

Future resilience

10% weight

Durability of demand and lifecycle risk

A high overall score should never hide a major weakness. For example, a fast-growing product might have poor margins, high return rates or a trend that is already fading. Keep the individual measurements visible alongside the total score.

## 6. The next research step

This smartphone report gives us a baseline. To analyze products for your own business, the next stage should examine a category where product-level sales, customer demand, pricing and future opportunities can be investigated in more detail.

My recommendation is to choose a category and then build a top-10 product intelligence report with:

1. The ten leading products, with the ranking period and data source.
2. Historical sales or demand trends and current momentum.
3. Customer needs, target segments and purchase drivers.
4. Pricing, competitor positioning and market gaps.
5. Risks, product lifecycle and a 12–24 month outlook.
6. Practical opportunities for our own product development or sales.

The smartphone report demonstrates the method; the next category should be selected based on what you want to build, sell or invest in.

## 3. A repeatable research schedule

- Daily: collect selected social trend signals and flag unusual spikes.
- Weekly: review rising search queries, emerging products and price changes.
- Monthly: export keyword estimates where available, compare countries, and update the opportunity scorecard.
- Quarterly: review product rankings, trade statistics, market changes and whether earlier hypotheses were confirmed.
- Every six months: reassess category coverage, source reliability and the ranking methodology.

Keep raw source files unchanged. Store collection time, period covered, geography, currency, units, source URL and any transformation applied. That allows you to reproduce the analysis and spot revisions in the original data.

## 4. Build the research database

Start with Google Sheets or CSV files. Do not build a complex application until the data collection process works reliably.

Use these six datasets:

| Dataset               | Required fields                                                               |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `categories`          | Category ID, name, parent category                                            |
| `products`            | Product name, brand, model, category, product URL                             |
| `search_observations` | Keyword, country, date range, Trends index, source                            |
| `market_observations` | Product, country, ranking, price, review count, sales indicator               |
| `trade_observations`  | Product classification, reporter country, year, import/export value, quantity |
| `research_hypotheses` | Claim, supporting evidence, confidence, test, outcome                         |

For prices, retain the original currency and store the exchange rate and conversion date separately. For rankings, record the exact ranking period. Never treat missing data as zero.

### Example of the evidence labels

Measured

Directly observed

Example: a marketplace listing displays a price of $49.99 on the collection date. This is an observed listing price, not necessarily the transaction price.

Estimated

Modelled or sampled

Example: a keyword tool estimates a monthly search volume of 10,000. Store the estimate, tool, geography and collection date; do not report it as an exact count.

Hypothesis

Interpretation requiring a test

Example: a rising trend combined with stable prices may indicate an opportunity for a differentiated product. Validate it with additional evidence before investing.

## 5. Metrics and ranking methodology

Use separate indicators rather than confusing demand, growth and profitability.

Demand

Measure search interest, available keyword estimates, marketplace rankings and any credible sales data. Keep the measurement units separate.

Momentum

Compare the most recent complete 30-day period with the preceding 30-day period, then validate against a longer historical baseline. Account for seasonality and incomplete periods.

Market attractiveness

Assess customer demand, competitor concentration, price range, supplier availability and barriers to entry.

Profitability

Estimate contribution per order:

\\[ \text{Contribution}=\text{Selling price}-\text{Variable costs} \\]

Include product cost, shipping, payment fees, fulfilment, expected returns and customer acquisition costs where applicable.

Confidence

Track source quality, freshness, geographic coverage and agreement between independent signals. A product with limited data should not rank as a proven opportunity.

For a first-pass opportunity score, normalize each metric to a 0–100 scale within a comparable category and geography:

\\[ \begin{aligned} S={}&0.25D+0.20M+0.15C\\\  &+0.15P+0.15R+0.10F \end{aligned} \\]

Where \\(D\\) is demand, \\(M\\) momentum, \\(C\\) customer value, \\(P\\) competition attractiveness, \\(R\\) profitability, and \\(F\\) future resilience.

These weights are proposed starting assumptions, not validated predictive coefficients. Define the component metrics carefully, invert competition measures where higher competition is undesirable, and test whether the score predicts successful outcomes. Apply a separate confidence rating rather than letting a high score compensate for unreliable data.

## 6. Implementation roadmap

1. Week 1 — Set up the research workspace

   Create the six datasets, select the country panel, define category classifications and collect baseline Google Trends exports.
2. Week 2 — Add demand and market signals

   Collect keyword estimates where accessible, inspect marketplace rankings and prices, and document coverage gaps. Begin with five categories and a manageable set of candidate products in each.
3. Week 3 — Compare and validate

   Calculate growth, seasonality, competition and profitability estimates. Investigate the strongest candidates manually and document alternative explanations.
4. Week 4 — Publish the first report

   Produce category-level top-ten lists, country comparisons, evidence links, confidence ratings and future hypotheses. Identify which data collection tasks are worth automating.
5. After the first month — Build the dashboard

   Move stable data sources into a Next.js/TypeScript application with PostgreSQL, scheduled ingestion, historical snapshots, charts, filtering and source-level evidence. Keep manual CSV import as a fallback.

## 7. The most important limitation

With a free-only budget, you can build a useful global demand and opportunity discovery system, but you cannot reliably produce a complete ranking of the world's top-selling products across every category using public data alone.

Google Trends measures relative search interest, while trade statistics measure international trade flows. Neither is a substitute for comprehensive consumer sales data. Google explicitly warns that Trends values are normalized and that unusual spikes can reflect noise rather than genuine demand.&#x20;
